# The Bite Response & Red-Flag Card™

## What to Do When Someone Gets Bitten — and When a Bite Is More Than a Bite

**Why this card exists:** In a record mosquito season, bites will happen even to
well-protected families. The difference between a bad week and a bad outcome is
knowing (a) how to soothe a bite properly, (b) how to watch for the common
complications, and (c) exactly which symptoms mean *call the doctor now*.
This card gives you all three — calmly, in plain English.

**The single most important sentence in this file:** Most mosquito bites are
unpleasant and harmless. A small fraction of people bitten by a West Nile–infected
mosquito develop serious illness, and the warning signs are specific and knowable.
This card teaches you to tell the difference. When in doubt, call your healthcare
provider — that is always the right answer.

---

## Part 1 — The First 10 Minutes After a Bite

**Step 1 — Wash it.** Clean the bite with soap and water. This removes mosquito
saliva residue and reduces the chance of the bite becoming infected. Pat dry.

**Step 2 — Cool it.** Apply a cold compress, ice pack wrapped in a cloth, or cool
damp cloth for 10 minutes. Cold shrinks the histamine response — the thing that
makes bites swell and itch.

**Step 3 — Stop the itch (before it starts).** The itch is your body's allergic
reaction to mosquito saliva. Attack it early:
- **Oral antihistamine** (per label, age-appropriate — e.g., cetirizine,
  loratadine, or diphenhydramine): the most effective tool for mosquito-bite itch,
  because it works from the inside
- **Hydrocortisone cream** (1%, OTC) on the bite: topical anti-inflammatory
- **Calamine lotion** or cooling gels (with pramoxine or menthol): soothing
- **Cool oatmeal bath** for multiple bites (kids especially love this one)

**Step 4 — Don't scratch. Seriously.** Scratching breaks skin, invites bacteria,
and turns a 3-day itch into a 10-day infection. For kids: trim nails short,
cover bites with a bandage or clothing if needed, and distract. For adults:
the hydrocortisone + antihistamine combo removes 90% of the urge.

**Step 5 — Mark the calendar.** Note the bite date. If you're in an area with
reported West Nile activity, the relevant symptom window is roughly **2–14 days**
after a bite (most commonly 2–6 days). Knowing the date keeps you calm and
informed instead of anxious and vague.

## Part 2 — The Normal Course (so you don't worry needlessly)

**What a normal bite looks like:**
- Small, red, swollen bump that appears within minutes to hours
- Itching that peaks in the first 24–48 hours
- Gradual fading over 3–7 days
- Some people (especially kids) get larger, warmer, more swollen reactions —
  this is often **skeeter syndrome**, a strong local allergic reaction. It looks
  alarming but is not dangerous by itself. Treat with the Part 1 protocol;
  if swelling is extreme or involves the face/eyes, call your provider.

**What a normal West Nile course looks like (if exposed):**
- About **4 in 5 people** infected with West Nile virus develop **no symptoms at all**
- About **1 in 5** develops a febrile illness: fever, headache, body aches, joint
  pain, vomiting, diarrhea, or a rash — usually resolving on its own over days to
  weeks (fatigue can linger)
- About **1 in 150** develops severe disease affecting the nervous system
  (see Part 3 — these are the red flags)

**The reassurance math:** even in an outbreak year, the *vast majority* of bites
are from uninfected mosquitoes, and the vast majority of infections are mild or
silent. You are watching for the rare severe pattern — not expecting it.

## Part 3 — The Red Flags: When to Call a Doctor (and When to Go Now)

**Call your healthcare provider promptly if, within 2–14 days of mosquito exposure
(especially with known West Nile activity in your area), you or a family member
develop:**

- Fever (especially with headache)
- Severe headache that feels different from your normal headaches
- Body aches, joint pain, or unusual fatigue
- Vomiting or diarrhea
- A rash (some West Nile patients develop a rash on the chest, back, or arms)

**Seek emergency care immediately if you or a family member develop:**

- **High fever with a stiff neck** (can't touch chin to chest) — a classic warning
  sign of neurological involvement
- **Confusion, disorientation, or trouble thinking clearly**
- **Severe headache with sensitivity to light**
- **Muscle weakness, tremors, or unsteady walking**
- **Numbness, tingling, or paralysis**
- **Seizures or convulsions**
- **Sudden vision loss or double vision**
- **Difficulty waking or staying awake**

**For children, add:** unusual lethargy (hard to wake, floppy), inconsolable
crying, poor feeding, or a bulging soft spot (infants).

**Why this matters:** severe West Nile disease (encephalitis or meningitis) is
serious and requires prompt medical evaluation. It is also rare. The point of this
card is not to frighten you — it's to make sure that *if* a red flag appears, you
recognize it in minutes, not days. There is no specific antiviral treatment for
West Nile; care is supportive, which is exactly why early recognition and
prevention matter so much.

## Part 4 — The "I Got Bit a Lot" Protocol

Had an unavoidable exposure — camping trip, evening event, forgotten repellent?
Run this for the next two weeks:

1. **Note the date** of exposure on the calendar (Day 0).
2. **Watch Days 2–14** for the Part 3 symptoms. Set a phone reminder for Day 5 and
   Day 10 — just a check-in: "Anyone feverish? Headache? Feeling off?"
3. **Don't self-diagnose on the internet.** If symptoms appear, call your provider
   and *tell them about the mosquito exposure and the date*. That information is
   clinically useful and they will know what to do.
4. **Keep protecting.** One exposure doesn't change the plan — the 4-Ring Defense™
   keeps working. Re-check your county's mosquito surveillance page weekly.
5. **Report dead birds** if your local health department requests it (some
   departments track West Nile through dead crow/jay reports). It helps your
   community and takes two minutes.

## Part 5 — Bite-Care Myths, Settled

| Myth | Truth |
|---|---|
| "Draw the poison out with heat/ammonia/bleach" | No. Heat worsens inflammation. Bleach/ammonia on skin is dangerous. Wash + cold + antihistamine |
| "Scratching an X into the bite helps" | No — it damages skin and raises infection risk |
| "If it's not itchy, it's not a mosquito bite" | False — reactions vary by person and species |
| "A big swollen bite means West Nile" | No — big local swelling is usually just an allergic reaction (skeeter syndrome). Watch for the systemic red flags in Part 3 instead |
| "Once you're bitten by one mosquito, you're immune to the itch" | False — reactions can change over time, and immunity to the virus is a separate matter entirely |
| "You only get bitten at night" | False — Aedes mosquitoes bite all day. Dawn and dusk are peak for most species, but daytime bites happen constantly (hence Ring 1, all day) |
